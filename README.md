# Rare Earth Elements

## Types

---

### **1. Light Rare Earth Elements (LREEs)**
Also known as the **Cerium Group**. They are more abundant and generally less valuable than HREEs.
*   **Lanthanum (La) - 57**: Used in camera lenses, catalysts for oil refining, and nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) batteries.
*   **Cerium (Ce) - 58**: The most abundant REE. Used in catalytic converters, glass polishing powders, and as a pigment.
*   **Praseodymium (Pr) - 59**: Used in high-strength permanent magnets (often combined with Nd), aircraft engines, and green glass/ceramic pigments.
*   **Neodymium (Nd) - 60**: Critical for the strongest permanent magnets (NdFeB) used in electric vehicle motors, wind turbines, and hard disk drives.
*   **Promethium (Pm) - 61**: Radioactive and synthetic (not found in significant quantities in nature). Used in nuclear batteries and luminescent paint.
*   **Samarium (Sm) - 62**: Used in SmCo permanent magnets (high-temperature stability), neutron capture in nuclear reactors, and cancer treatment.

### **2. Heavy Rare Earth Elements (HREEs)**
Also known as the **Yttrium Group**. They are less abundant, often more critical for high-tech applications, and command higher prices.
*   **Europium (Eu) - 63**: Key for red and blue phosphors in LED lights, fluorescent lamps, and TV/computer screens.
*   **Gadolinium (Gd) - 64**: Used in MRI contrast agents, neutron shielding, and data storage media.
*   **Terbium (Tb) - 65**: Used in green phosphors, solid-state devices, and as an additive to Neodymium-based magnets to improve high-temperature performance.
*   **Dysprosium (Dy) - 66**: **Critically important** as an additive to Neodymium magnets to maintain strength at high temperatures, essential for EV motors and wind turbines.
*   **Holmium (Ho) - 67**: Has the highest magnetic strength of any element. Used in nuclear control rods and solid-state lasers.
*   **Erbium (Er) - 68**: Used in fiber-optic cable amplifiers, laser applications (medical/dental), and pink glass pigments.
*   **Thulium (Tm) - 69**: Used in portable X-ray machines and high-efficiency lasers.
*   **Ytterbium (Yb) - 70**: Used in stainless steel, industrial lasers, and as a doping agent in optical fibers.
*   **Lutetium (Lu) - 71**: The heaviest and hardest lanthanide. Used in catalysts for petroleum cracking, LED light bulbs, and cancer therapy.

### **3. The "Outliers"**
Often included because they are found in the same ore deposits and have similar chemical properties.
*   **Scandium (Sc) - 21**: Technically a light element but not a lanthanide. Used in high-performance aluminum-scandium alloys for aerospace and sports equipment.
*   **Yttrium (Y) - 39**: Chemically similar to HREEs and always grouped with them. **Crucial** for red phosphors in TV/display screens and as a stabilizer in zirconia ceramics.

**Geological Note:** In mineral deposits, REEs are always found mixed together. The challenge of modern refining is to efficiently separate these very chemically similar elements. The "heavies" (HREEs + Yttrium) are often of greatest strategic concern due to their concentrated supply chain and irreplaceable roles in clean energy and defense technologies.

### Documented and Candidate Species in North America

**1. *Helianthus annuus* (Common Sunflower)**
*   **Type:** Annual flowering plant (Asteraceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Broad-spectrum uptake of Light REEs (La, Ce, Nd).
*   **Notes:** Not a true hyperaccumulator by strict definition, but extensively studied for **phytoremediation** due to its fast growth, high biomass, and proven ability to extract various metals, including REEs, from contaminated soils and water (hydroponics). A leading candidate for applied phytomining trials in North America.

**2. *Pteridium aquilinum* (Bracken Fern)**
*   **Type:** Perennial fern.
*   **Key REEs:** Primarily Light REEs (Ce, La).
*   **Notes:** A cosmopolitan fern common across North America. Studies (including those in the US) have shown it can accumulate REEs, often in conjunction with arsenic. Its widespread presence on disturbed or acidic soils makes it a relevant indicator species.

**3. *Athyrium filix-femina* (Lady Fern) & *Dryopteris* spp. (Wood Ferns)**
*   **Type:** Perennial ferns.
*   **Key REEs:** Various REEs.
*   **Notes:** Ferns, in general, possess a natural propensity for REE uptake. These common native ferns in North American forests are likely accumulators, especially when growing on mineralized substrates, though targeted phytomining research is less common than in Asia.

**4. *Salix* spp. (Willows)**
*   **Type:** Fast-growing trees/shrubs.
*   **Key REEs:** Broad-spectrum uptake.
*   **Notes:** Willows are well-established **phytoxtractors** used in bioremediation for other metals. Research (including Canadian studies) demonstrates their ability to take up REEs into their roots and woody biomass, making them candidates for **rhizofiltration** and stabilizing REE-laden waste sites.

**5. *Betula papyrifera* (Paper Birch) & *Populus tremuloides* (Trembling Aspen)**
*   **Type:** Deciduous trees.
*   **Key REEs:** Predominantly Light REEs.
*   **Notes:** Studies on forest biogeochemistry in Canada (e.g., around unusual geological formations or old mine sites) have shown these widespread trees can bioaccumulate REEs in their leaves, acting as broad-scale biogeochemical indicators for mineral exploration.

**6. Plants from Unique Geochemical Provinces:**
*   **Location:** **Iron Hill, Colorado (USA)** - a carbonatite intrusion rich in REEs.
    *   **Species:** Native vegetation, including various forbs and grasses, has been found with elevated REE concentrations, demonstrating natural adaptation.
*   **Location:** **Serpentine Soils (e.g., in Quebec/California)** - naturally high in chromium, nickel, and sometimes associated elements.
    *   **Species:** **Serpentine-endemic flora** (e.g., certain *Streptanthus* species in CA) are adapted to harsh, metal-rich soils. While studied for Ni hyperaccumulation, their interaction with co-located REEs is a potential research area.

### Key European REE-Accumulating Species

**1. *Arabidopsis halleri* (Haller's Rockcress)**
*   **Type:** Perennial herb (Brassicaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Light REEs (La, Ce, Nd).
*   **Notes:** A well-known **zinc and cadmium hyperaccumulator** model species. Research, particularly in Central Europe (Germany, Austria), has confirmed its co-accumulation of REEs when grown on contaminated or mineralized soils. It is a primary European species for phytoremediation research.

**2. *Noccaea caerulescens* (Alpine Pennycress)**
*   **Type:** Perennial herb (Brassicaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Broad-spectrum REEs.
*   **Notes:** Formerly *Thlaspi caerulescens*. One of the most famous and studied **metal hyperaccumulators** in the world (for Zn, Cd, Ni). Studies in France and Switzerland have demonstrated its significant capacity to accumulate REEs alongside its primary target metals, making it a key candidate for phytomining on multi-contaminated European sites.

**3. *Pteridium aquilinum* (Bracken Fern)**
*   **Type:** Perennial fern.
*   **Key REEs:** Primarily Light REEs (Ce, La).
*   **Notes:** This cosmopolitan fern is ubiquitous across European heathlands and acidic forests. Studies in the UK and Scandinavia have shown it acts as a **passive accumulator** of REEs, often with high bioconcentration factors, making it a relevant biogeochemical indicator plant.

**4. *Calluna vulgaris* (Heather/Common Ling)**
*   **Type:** Evergreen shrub (Ericaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Light and Heavy REEs.
*   **Notes:** A dominant plant on acidic, nutrient-poor soils (heathlands, moors) across Europe. Research in Germany and the UK indicates it accumulates REEs, likely due to its association with mycorrhizal fungi and adaptations for nutrient uptake in harsh, often mineral-rich substrates.

**5. *Plantago lanceolata* (Ribwort Plantain)**
*   **Type:** Perennial herb (Plantaginaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Broad-spectrum uptake.
*   **Notes:** A common grassland species studied in Italy and Central Europe for its metal tolerance. It has been identified as a **good candidate for phytostabilization** and shows measurable uptake of REEs, particularly in soils impacted by industrial activity or natural geochemical anomalies.

**6. Species from Unique European Geochemical Sites:**
*   **Location: Norra Kärr (Sweden)** - an alkaline complex with HREE enrichment.
    *   **Species:** Native flora, including mosses, grasses, and **conifer saplings**, have been studied for their REE uptake patterns as part of environmental impact assessments for potential mining.
*   **Location: Vergenoeg (France)** & other **Peatlands/Bogs**.
    *   **Species: *Sphagnum* mosses** and other bog plants can strongly bind and accumulate REEs from groundwater, creating a natural biogeochemical archive. Peat cores are used to trace historical REE deposition.
*   **Location: Serpentine Soils (e.g., Balkans, Italy, Norway)**.
    *   **Species:** **Nickel hyperaccumulators** endemic to these soils (e.g., *Alyssum bertolonii* in Italy, *Bornmuellera tymphaea* in Greece) are likely to interact with and accumulate co-located REEs, though this is a secondary research focus.

### Key European Context & Research Gap:
*   **No "Super-Accumulator":** Europe lacks a well-documented, dominant **hyperaccumulator specialist for REEs** comparable to Asia's *Dicranopteris*. Research has focused more on **facultative accumulators**—plants known for other metals (Zn, Cd, Ni) that also take up REEs.
*   **Focus on Phytoremediation:** The driving research is often **environmental remediation** of historic mine sites, industrial brownfields, or soils with legacy contamination, rather than primary phytomining.
*   **Natural Laboratories:** Unique geological sites like the **HREE-rich Norra Kärr in Sweden** or **ultramafic (serpentine) regions** in the Balkans provide critical natural laboratories for studying native plant adaptation.
*   **Indicator Use:** Many common European species (ferns, heathers) are valued as **bioindicators** for monitoring environmental pollution and mapping geochemical anomalies.

**Conclusion:** Europe's REE-accumulating flora consists largely of **metal-tolerant generalists and passive accumulators** found in acidic, nutrient-poor, or metalliferous environments. The most significant species are the well-studied metallophytes from the Brassicaceae family (*Noccaea*, *Arabidopsis*), with ongoing research expanding the list across different biogeographic regions.

### Documented and Probable African REE-Accumulating Species

*   **Type:** Perennial herb (Asteraceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Likely broad-spectrum (inference from other metals).
*   **Notes:** A famous **nickel hyperaccumulator** endemic to ultramafic soils in South Africa (Mpumalanga). While studied extensively for Ni phytomining, its interaction with REEs (which can co-occur in these serpentine soils) is a logical and promising area for research. It is Africa's premier model species for metal hyperaccumulation.

**2. *Silene burchelli* (Burchell's Catchfly)**
*   **Type:** Perennial herb (Caryophyllaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Documented for Light REEs (La, Ce, Nd).
*   **Notes:** This is one of the few **directly documented** REE accumulators in Africa. Research from **South Africa** has shown this species accumulates high concentrations of REEs, particularly in its shoots, when growing on REE-enriched soils from old mine dumps or geological anomalies.

**3. *Geniosporum tenuiflorum* (Syn. *Hemizygia welwitschii*)**
*   **Type:** Shrub (Lamiaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Documented for Light and Heavy REEs.
*   **Notes:** A key species identified in early biogeochemical exploration studies in **Malawi**. It was found to be a strong indicator plant for REE mineralization, with leaves showing marked enrichment. It represents classic African "geobotany" applied to mineral prospecting.

**4. *Pteridium aquilinum* (Bracken Fern)**
*   **Type:** Perennial fern.
*   **Key REEs:** Primarily Light REEs (Ce, La).
*   **Notes:** This globally widespread fern is present across Africa. Its known propensity to accumulate REEs makes it a probable and widespread accumulator on the continent, especially in disturbed, acidic, or mineralized areas.

**5. Plants from African Carbonatite Complexes (Prime Locations):**
    Carbonatite intrusions are the world's primary source of REEs. Africa hosts several major complexes (e.g., Mountain Pass in the USA is an outlier).
    *   **Location: *The Chilwa Alkaline Province* (Malawi, Mozambique)**
        *   **Species:** Native shrubs and trees (e.g., ***Pteleopsis myrtifolia***) have been studied since the mid-20th century as **biogeochemical indicators** for REE and niobium mineralization. They show clear uptake signatures.
    *   **Location: *Lofdal, Namibia* (HREE-rich carbonatite)**
        *   **Species:** Native vegetation is currently being studied as part of exploration and environmental baseline projects. Specific accumulator species are likely but not yet widely published.
    *   **Location: *Kangankunde, Malawi* (One of the world's highest-grade REE deposits)**
        *   **Species:** The unique flora on this carbonate-rich hill has been noted for decades. Research is active to identify the best indicator and accumulator plants among the local grasses and shrubs.

**6. Species from the Katanga Copper Belt (DRC & Zambia):**
*   **Type:** Various "copper flowers" (e.g., *Haumaniastrum katangense*, *Aeolanthus biformifolius*).
*   **Key REEs:** Probable co-accumulation.
*   **Notes:** While world-famous as **copper and cobalt hyperaccumulators**, the unique soils of this region are also polymetallic. The interaction of these specialized plants with REEs is a virtually untapped but highly promising research field.

### Critical African Context & Research Gap:
*   **Massive Potential, Limited Studies:** Africa has the **geological endowment** (major carbonatites, alkaline complexes, unique metallogenic belts) but lacks the concentrated **phytomining research investment** seen in China. This is the single largest gap in global knowledge on the subject.
*   **Focus on Geobotanical Prospecting:** Historically, the most significant work has been in **mineral exploration** (e.g., 1960s-80s in Malawi), using plants to find ore bodies, not to extract metals. This provides a crucial foundation.
*   **Untapped Hyperaccumulator Diversity:** Africa is a hotspot for **metal-tolerant flora** (especially for Ni, Co, Cu, Zn). The **Brassicaceae, Asteraceae, and Lamiaceae** families, which contain many global hyperaccumulators, are well-represented. It is statistically certain that undiscovered REE specialists exist.
*   **Practical Implications:** Identifying African REE accumulators could lead to **low-impact phytomining** on tailings from historical mines or as part of site remediation, offering a sustainable development pathway for mineral-rich regions.

**Conclusion:** Africa is not lacking in REE-accumulating plants; it is lacking in the research to systematically identify and characterize them. The confirmed examples (**Silene burchelli, Geniosporum**) and the high-potential candidates (from carbonatite floras and famous metallophytes like *Berkheya coddii*) point to a significant and underexplored scientific frontier.

*   **Notes:** Widely planted and naturalized across the Mediterranean Middle East (e.g., Israel, Lebanon, Turkey). Research in **Turkey** and the Mediterranean basin has shown oleander to be an effective **phytoremediator** for various heavy metals and a documented accumulator of REEs from contaminated soils, making it a practical choice for landscaping polluted areas.

**4. *Pistacia lentiscus* (Mastic Tree) & *Pistacia atlantica* (Atlantic Pistachio)**
*   **Type:** Drought-tolerant trees/shrubs (Anacardiaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** Probable (based on biogeochemical studies).
*   **Notes:** Keystone species of Mediterranean and Irano-Turanian woodlands. In **Turkey** and **Iran**, *Pistacia* species have been used in **biogeochemical prospecting** studies for various metals. Their deep root systems and presence on diverse geological substrates make them likely indicators for soil REE anomalies.

**5. Plants from the Arabian Shield (Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman):**
    The Precambrian rocks of the Shield host various mineralizations.
    *   **Species:** Endemic, drought-resistant flora of the **'Nafud'** (sand deserts) and rocky **'harrats'** (lava fields). Specific species are undescribed in this context, but hardy shrubs like ***Calotropis procera* (Apple of Sodom)** and acacias (***Vachellia* spp.**) are widespread and could be studied as pioneer species on mineralized ground.

**6. Species from Turkish & Iranian Metallogenic Provinces:**
    More research exists here due to longer-established scientific institutions.
    *   **Location: Turkey** – Areas with serpentine soils and boron-rich mineral deposits.
        *   **Species:** Some Turkish *Alyssum* species (Brassicaceae) are known **nickel hyperaccumulators**. Their interaction with REEs on ultramafics is probable but unverified.
    *   **Location: Iran** – Various large porphyry copper and zinc-lead deposits.
        *   **Species:** Research has identified certain native *Astragalus* species (Fabaceae) and *Artemisia* species (Asteraceae) as strong accumulators of heavy metals (like Pb, Zn), suggesting potential for REE uptake in co-contaminated soils.

### Critical Middle Eastern Context & Major Research Gap:
*   **Vast Underexplored Potential:** This is arguably the **least studied region globally** for REE phytomining, despite having the **relevant geology** (phosphates, shield rocks) and **plant adaptations** (xerophytic, halophytic species suited to harsh, mineralized soils).
*   **Phosphate Link is Key:** The most direct pathway for discovery is studying plants on **phosphate mining overburden and tailings** in North Africa and the Middle East. This is a major commercial and environmental opportunity.
*   **Focus on Traditional Phytoremediation:** Existing research (e.g., in Turkey, Iran) focuses on heavy metals (lead, cadmium, boron) from mining, with REEs as a secondary consideration.
*   **Logistical Challenges:** Political instability, security concerns, and funding priorities in many parts of the region have likely hindered systematic field botany and biogeochemical surveys.

**Conclusion:** The Middle East likely hosts unique, drought-adapted REE accumulators, particularly on **phosphate-bearing lands** and the **Arabian Shield**, but they remain virtually unidentified by science. The most promising leads are **xerophytic shrubs on mine wastes** and documented phytoremediators like **oleander**. This region represents one of the final major frontiers in global hyperaccumulator plant discovery.

Excellent—we return to the **epicenter of research and discovery**. **Asia**, particularly **Southern China**, is the global hotspot for the study and identification of REE hyperaccumulators. This is due to a combination of unique acidic soils, extensive weathered REE deposits, and a concentrated research focus on phytoremediation and agromining.

Here is the consolidated list of key **Asian** species, expanding on our initial discussion with more regional detail.

### Premier Asian REE Hyperaccumulators

**1. *Dicranopteris dichotoma* (Old World Forked Fern)**
*   **Type:** Perennial fern (Gleicheniaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** **LREEs (Nd, Ce, La)** predominantly, but can absorb a range.
*   **Notes:** **The world's most famous and studied REE hyperaccumulator.** It forms dense "fern crusts" on highly weathered, acidic soils (laterites) in Southern China's REE mining regions (e.g., Jiangxi, Fujian). It is the primary model species for understanding REE uptake mechanisms and the leading candidate for **commercial phytomining** in Asia.

**2. *Phytolacca americana* (American Pokeweed)**
*   **Type:** Perennial herb (Phytolaccaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** **LREEs (La, Ce)** with high translocation to shoots.
*   **Notes:** An invasive species in Asia that has become a **star in phytoremediation research**. Studies in China show it has an extraordinary capacity to accumulate REEs, especially from contaminated soils, with high biomass yield. It's a top candidate for practical cleanup applications.

**3. *Pronephrium simplex* & *Lygodium japonicum* (Ferns)**
*   **Type:** Perennial ferns.
*   **Key REEs:** *P. simplex* for **LREEs**; *L. japonicum* for a broader range, including **HREEs**.
*   **Notes:** These ferns are common in the same ecosystems as *Dicranopteris*. *Lygodium japonicum* (Japanese Climbing Fern) is noted for its relative efficiency in absorbing the more valuable Heavy REEs.

**4. *Mosla chinensis* (Chinese Mosla)**
*   **Type:** Annual herb (Lamiaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** **HREEs (Yttrium-Y, in particular)**.
*   **Notes:** A significant find because it shows a **preferential accumulation for Heavy REEs**, which are economically more critical and scarce. This makes it a highly valuable species for targeted phytomining research.

**5. *Pteris vittata* (Chinese Ladder Brake Fern)**
*   **Type:** Perennial fern (Pteridaceae).
*   **Key REEs:** **LREEs (Ce, La)**.
*   **Notes:** Globally famous as an **arsenic hyperaccumulator**. Its co-accumulation of REEs, especially in soils co-contaminated with arsenic and REEs, makes it a powerful tool for multi-element remediation.

### Other Notable Asian Accumulators

*   ***Sedum alfredii* (Jingxi Stonecrop):** A succulent known for hyperaccumulating cadmium and zinc; also co-accumulates REEs (Ce, La).
*   ***Camellia sinensis* (Tea Plant):** Studies in mining areas show it can absorb REEs into its leaves, raising concerns about tea safety but also indicating its uptake capacity.
*   ***Clerodendrum mandarinorum* (Glorybower):** A shrub identified in Chinese screening studies as a potential REE accumulator.
*   ***Illicium lanceolatum* (Star Anise):** An evergreen tree studied in China for its REE accumulation potential from soils.

### Why Asia is the Global Leader:
*   **Perfect Natural Laboratory:** The **ion-adsorption clay deposits** of Southern China are weathered, acidic, and have REEs in a bioavailable form, creating ideal conditions for plants to evolve uptake mechanisms.
*   **Major Environmental Driver:** Intensive REE mining has caused significant environmental damage, spurring massive government and academic investment in **phytoremediation solutions**.
*   **Systematic Screening:** Chinese institutions have conducted the world's most extensive screenings of native flora around mine sites, leading to the discovery of multiple "champion" species.
*   **Applied Research Focus:** The step from discovery to application (phytomining trials) is more advanced here than anywhere else in the world.

**Conclusion:** Asia, led by China, is home to the most effective and well-studied REE hyperaccumulators on the planet. The species listed above, particularly the ferns (*Dicranopteris*, *Pteris*) and the herb *Phytolacca*, represent the cutting edge of both the science and potential practical application of REE phytomining. Research here continues to define the field globally.
